<div class="lede" data-levels="public" markdown="1">
Many teachers learned science from textbooks and lectures and have never done research themselves. In the summer of 2015, a University of Wyoming team gave 23 teachers two weeks, two telescopes, and a team of astronomers, and asked each small group to pose and answer a research question of its own. The teachers began excited, sank into confusion in the middle, and finished ready to bring astronomy into their classrooms. This page lets you try their projects and read what they said along the way.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
Professional development (PD) is how working teachers keep learning. LASSI (Launching Astronomy: Standards and STEM Integration) was a two-week summer PD in which 23 teachers, most of them in elementary schools, did authentic science: real questions, real telescopes, and real data, with professional astronomers nearby. The study tracked what the teachers found hard, what they gained, and how much astronomy they learned. Below, you can run the same calculations the teachers ran, from weighing Jupiter to measuring how fast distant galaxies move away.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
A mixed-methods study of one authentic-science PD for pre-collegiate educators (n = 23; 17 elementary, six secondary), read through a social constructionist lens. Mornings went to eight team research projects on two research telescopes, afternoons to content sessions, and the last day to a community poster session. Interviews, an anonymous community notebook, and field notes trace an engaged, disengaged, and re-engaged arc; eight pre-to-post content tests all improved (p < .05). The page centers that study and gives the teachers' astronomy one demo.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="grad" markdown="1">
Three research questions (successes and challenges, perceptions before and after, and content gains) were answered with qualitative coding (Creswell, 2014) triangulated across three sources, a nine-point perception survey adapted from agricultural education, and eight researcher-made pre-to-post tests. All eight tests rejected the null at p < .05, five at p < .00001, with d ≥ 0.8 on all eight. The per-test values sit in Table 3, which this page could not read, so at this level the page works out what those thresholds require of a paired test with 23 teachers.
</div>

## Two weeks at LASSI

<div id="as-design" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
Each morning, eight small teams worked on research questions they chose themselves. Each afternoon, the whole group learned astronomy and physics content, from the light spectrum to Hubble's law. In the first week, teams could observe every evening through a campus telescope, and a few teachers at a time traveled to a mountaintop observatory at night. On the last day, every team presented a poster to the community, the way scientists share results.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
Look at the "Who came" bars: 17 of the 23 teachers taught elementary school (ages five to ten), and 21 taught science. The team invited about 500 STEM teachers by email from up to 645 kilometers (about 400 miles) away. Seven experts supported the work, five of them active astronomy researchers, so teachers worked beside people who do this research for a living.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug grad res" markdown="1">
The design flips a typical PD day: open-ended "expert time" on self-generated questions in the morning, structured content in the afternoon. Teams used two facilities: STAR, a 16-inch Schmidt-Cassegrain on the roof of the University of Wyoming Physical Sciences building, and WIRO, the 2.4 m Wyoming Infrared Observatory on Jelm Mountain at 2943.1 m (9656 ft). Partnership support included expert consultation, archival data, Stellarium, and Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) spectra. Clouds on several WIRO nights and the time needed to collect data shaped what each team could finish, as the quotes below show.
</div>

## The teachers' research

<div id="as-projects" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
The paper describes four of the eight team projects. Pick one. **The mass of Jupiter** lets you watch Jupiter's four largest moons swing back and forth, the view the teachers had through the telescope, and turns one moon's orbit into Jupiter's mass. **Angular size** shows how a coin shrinks with distance. **Classifying spectra** asks you to sort nine rainbows of light into stars, galaxies, and quasars (the bright centers of distant galaxies), and **Hubble's law** turns the shift in that light into a distance.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
Kepler's third law links a moon's orbit to the planet it circles: P² = a³ when the period P is in years, the orbit size a is in astronomical units (Earth–Sun distances), and the mass comes out in Suns. Move the error sliders and watch a small mistake grow: mass depends on the cube of the orbit size, so a 5 percent error there becomes about a 16 percent error in Jupiter's mass. The teachers repeated the measurement with all four moons for exactly this reason.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug grad res" markdown="1">
Every calculator uses the paper's equations (P² = a³, a = L/D, v/c = (λ − λ₀)/λ₀, and d = v/H₀), but the paper reports methods and outcomes rather than the teams' measured values, so every number shown is the page's. The moon orbits are real; the spectra are simulated, because the WIRO data are not published. The paper names H₀ only as "the current best-measured value," so the page offers three. <span data-levels="grad res">The paper applies the low-redshift form v = cz to its three quasars as well as its galaxies; at most of the quasar redshifts simulated here, z exceeds one and the formula gives speeds above c, which the relativistic column corrects.</span>
</div>

## The middle dip

<div id="as-arc" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public hs" markdown="1">
The teachers' feelings followed a pattern the paper names: excitement at first, then confusion and frustration in the middle as open-ended research met cloudy nights and unclear expectations, then renewed engagement as projects came together. On day six, one teacher called the approach "disconnect[ed] and highly unorganized." By day ten, another wrote of "an excitement for teaching astronomy concepts." The team read the anonymous notebook every day and adjusted the next day's plan.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug grad res" markdown="1">
The paper describes the sequence as dissatisfaction with the open-ended project, then acceptance with disengagement, then engagement and excitement, and recommends that PD teams plan for the middle "dip" rather than avoid it: explain objectives, process, and outcomes before, during, and after, and communicate successes and challenges openly. The anonymous community notebook served as both a data source and a daily feedback channel; the team met each day to review entries and adjust. The figure's shading follows that description and carries no measured score; Figure 1 of the paper places selected quotes on a timeline.
</div>

## In their words

<div id="as-voices" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public hs" markdown="1">
Table 1 of the paper collects three sample quotes for each of eight themes, one from each data source. Switch to "What they asked for" and the pattern is clear: teachers wanted more guidance, more ready-made activities, and more ways to use the content with young students. Switch to "What they gained" and the same teachers describe lesson plans they built, time with experts, and how much they learned without noticing.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug grad res" markdown="1">
Teachers named three benefits (content to take back, the lesson plans they created, and interaction with experts) and four concerns (project guidance, time management, teaching the content, and using project information in their classrooms). They also asked for less open-ended inquiry and more concrete, classroom-ready activities, even though the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) call for more inquiry. The perception survey adds that teachers saw authentic science as a way to prepare students more effectively and saw supports (parents and administrators) rather than barriers; those values sit in Table 2, which this page could not read.
</div>

## What the tests showed

<div id="as-tests" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public hs" markdown="1">
Before and after each content session, teachers took a short quiz the team wrote. Scores rose on all eight quizzes, by more than chance would explain, and five of the eight rose by a margin that, if the sessions had changed nothing, chance would produce less than once in 100,000 tries. The effect on every quiz was large by the usual yardstick for effect size.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
Each test compared the same teachers before and after a session, so a paired t test fits, and the paper reports significance and Cohen's d. The text lists seven topic labels for eight tests, from foundations of computing to Hubble's law and remote observing; the mapping of labels to tests, and each test's values, are in Table 3 and Figure 2.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
With 23 teachers, a two-sided paired test reaches p < .00001 only when |t| ≥ 5.69 on 22 degrees of freedom, that is, a standardized mean change d<sub>z</sub> ≥ 1.19. If the paper's d is d<sub>z</sub>, then d ≥ 0.8 alone implies t ≥ 3.84 and p ≤ 0.0009, so the p < .05 result follows from the effect size. If d is standardized by the pre and post standard deviations (d<sub>av</sub>), the relation depends on the pre-post correlation, which the sliders let you vary. These computations are the page's; the paper does not state n per test or its standardizer.
</div>

## What the paper found

<div data-levels="public hs" markdown="1">
- Teachers moved from excitement, to a middle stretch of confusion, to renewed engagement.
- Teachers wanted clear expectations and concrete activities before the research began.
- Teachers valued the experts, the lesson plans they built, and content they could take back.
- Scores rose on all eight content quizzes, with large effects.
- Teachers saw authentic science as a way to prepare their students better.
- The team concluded that teachers need experience, instruction, scaffolding, and continued support to bring authentic science into classrooms.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug grad res" markdown="1">
- **An engagement arc.** Engaged at first, disengaged in the middle, re-engaged by the end; dated quotes on days three, six, and ten.
- **A wish for certainty.** Teachers asked for content and deliverables up front and for less open-ended inquiry, even as the standards call for more.
- **Benefits and concerns.** Benefits: content, created lesson plans, and expert interaction. Concerns: guidance, time, teaching the content, and classroom use.
- **Content gains.** Eight of eight tests at p < .05, five at p < .00001, and d ≥ 0.8 on all eight.
- **Perceptions.** Authentic science seen as better preparation for students; supports outweighed barriers; teacher preparation should model authentic science.
- **Design recommendation.** Expert structure and support for question generation, data collection, and analysis, with explanation before, during, and after.
</div>

## Why it matters

<div data-levels="public hs" markdown="1">
Science standards ask teachers to let students investigate real questions, and many teachers have never done research themselves. This study shows what happens when they do: the work is hard, the middle feels lost, and the finish builds both knowledge and confidence. Ultimately, the lesson for anyone who trains teachers is to expect the dip, explain it, and support people through it.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
National data cited in the paper show that most secondary science teachers hold a degree in their field (about 77 percent of roughly 127,000) and that the share drops at the middle and elementary levels, where 17 of these 23 teachers worked. For many of them, a PD like LASSI is the only chance to do research before teaching it. The paper turns that chance into design guidance: anticipate the stages of inquiry, keep experts close, and connect every step to the classroom.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
The study documents the affective cost of authentic inquiry for teachers and proposes a response (scaffolded explanation, daily feedback through an anonymous notebook, and sustained expert partnership) that PD teams can adopt and test. Its future work names larger samples, more diverse research topics, pre-service experiences, a closer study of the mid-process disengagement, and shorter expert-supported experiences that others can adopt; the companion 2021 paper follows the same team across three summers.
</div>

## Notes from building this page

- **Two pages were not readable.** The journal PDF's extracted text runs from page 67 to page 70; Tables 2 and 3 and Figures 1 and 2 fall in the gap. The page therefore shows the stated test summary without per-test values and does not draw the perception survey. A reachable copy of pages 68 and 69 would let a later revision draw them.
- **What the thresholds require.** At n = 23, p < .00001 needs |t| ≥ 5.69 (d<sub>z</sub> ≥ 1.19), and d<sub>z</sub> = 0.8 alone gives p = 0.0009. Naming the standardizer (d<sub>z</sub> or d<sub>av</sub>) and n per test would let readers connect the two reported thresholds. These numbers are the page's.
- **Seven labels, eight tests.** The results list seven topic labels for eight tests; Table 3 presumably maps them.
- **The companion paper counts the summer differently.** The 2021 Education Sciences paper reports 22 teachers for 2015 LASSI (21 sampled; 16 female; 16 elementary) and seven content topics, five at p < 0.0001 and two (Galileoscopes, and the Doppler effect with angular size) not significant. This paper reports 23 teachers (18 female; 17 elementary) and eight tests, all at p < .05. Different analysis windows or item groupings may explain the difference; the page does not choose between them.
- **Low-redshift Hubble's law for quasars.** The paper applies v/c = (λ − λ₀)/λ₀ and d = v/H₀ to galaxies and quasars alike; for quasars at z near or above one, a relativistic velocity and a cosmological distance would be the standard treatment. A note in teacher materials would keep the classroom version from producing speeds above c.
- **Survey source.** The perception survey is described as adapted from an agricultural education survey with a nine-point scale; its citation and anchors were not in the readable text.

## Try this in class

1. In teams, open **The mass of Jupiter**, pick a moon, and record its farthest swing (in Jupiter diameters) and its period. Compute Jupiter's mass with M = a³ ÷ P² and compare with your classmates' moons.
2. Set the orbit-size error to 5 percent, then the period error to 5 percent, and record how much the mass changes each time. Write one sentence explaining why the orbit-size error matters more.
3. Hold a team "poster minute": each team shares its best estimate, its biggest source of error, and one thing that felt confusing in the middle. Compare that last answer with the teachers' day-six quote.
