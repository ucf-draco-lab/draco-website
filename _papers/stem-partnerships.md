<div class="lede" data-levels="public" markdown="1">
Schools and universities are often encouraged to team up so that engineers can bring engineering into secondary classrooms. This paper looks closely at three of those teams. In 2010–2011, an engineering graduate student worked about 20 hours a week with two teachers at an urban high school. In 2018–2019, two teams of three college students each built a payload for a high-altitude balloon and turned it into lessons at two rural schools. The researchers asked what "partnership" meant to each person and how the partners worked things out. The team that spent the most time together built the weakest partnership. Start by guessing which team built the strongest.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
Andrea C. Borowczak, Trina Johnson Kilty, and Mike Borowczak compared three teams that brought engineering into middle and high school classrooms. One team was an engineer and two teachers at an urban school (2010–2011). The other two were college students, each team pairing an engineering major, a physics major, and a future teacher, who sent experiments up on a high-altitude balloon and taught lessons about them (2018–2019). The researchers watched classes, interviewed everyone, read their emails, and sorted each team onto a three-step ladder of partnership. Below, you can guess the ranking, walk through the study, see how the partners settled (or skipped) their disagreements, and fly the balloon one team taught with.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
The paper compares two qualitative case studies of school–university engineering partnerships, nearly a decade apart, through a socio-cultural lens. Case 1 is a triad (an engineering Ph.D. student and two teachers, with an educational liaison) that taught five engineering lessons in an urban secondary school over the 2010–2011 school year. Case 2 is two undergraduate teams that built high-altitude balloon payloads and taught four and five lessons at two rural schools in 2018–2019. Data are focus groups, interviews, field notes, and emails, coded for what partnership meant and how partners negotiated the work. Teams are placed on Mullinix's three levels: pre-partnership, partnership with a little p, and Partnership with a big P.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="grad" markdown="1">
A cross-case qualitative comparison of three partnership teams across two studies (Case 1: one urban triad, 2010–2011, analyzed with open, axial, and selective coding; Case 2: two undergraduate teams of three, 2018–2019). Research questions: what partnership meant to each participant, and how individuals negotiated the work of engineering lessons. The authors find four themes of partnership meaning (lesson creation, communication, engineering content, and student learning), few complete negotiations, and partnership levels that tracked lesson strength rather than time invested. The paper reports no counts of coded segments and no statistics; the page presents its evidence as the paper does and keeps the one computed demo (balloon physics) separate and labeled.
</div>

<div class="lede" data-levels="res" markdown="1">
Borowczak, Kilty, and Borowczak (Education Sciences 2025) apply Mullinix's (2001) partnership continuum and a Goodlad-derived definition of partnership ("a planned, mutually beneficial, two or more party relationship") to two cases collected under separate protocols nearly a decade apart. The contribution is descriptive: one team reached a little-p partnership, two did not, and the strongest predictor visible in the evidence is relational (role clarity, trust, feedback), with hours together pointing the other way. The page reconstructs the participant tables from the text, lists every reported negotiation episode, and closes the researcher level with notes on the design, internal inconsistencies, and what a cross-decade comparison of different team types can support.
</div>

## Which team built the strongest partnership?

<div id="sp-ladder" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
Pick the team you think worked best together. The ladder that appears has three rungs. On the bottom rung, people cooperate but mostly work on their own. On the middle rung, they work toward goals they all value, with roles they agreed on. The top rung, a full Partnership, was not reached by anyone in this study. The Coleman team spent about 20 hours a week together, the most of any team, and still sat on the bottom rung. Time alone did not make a partnership.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
Look at the evidence rows for each team. The Cosmic Radiation team met every week, gave each person a clear job, and let students race two shielded payloads against each other. The Speed of Sound team split up: the future teacher said the others "kicked me out of their meetings," and the students "did not build or touch anything that went into space." At Coleman, everyone called it a partnership, but one teacher said decisions were not shared, and the engineer never asked for the feedback he wanted.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
The paper adopts Mullinix's three levels and describes them through the cases rather than with a scoring rubric. Pre-partnership means limited, specifically defined relationships, early trust, and autonomous work; a little-p partnership means working toward "mutually valued objectives," which in this study included a signed agreement on roles. The three teams differ on exactly those markers: role clarity and trust separate Cosmic Radiation from the other two, while hours together do not.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
The placements are the authors' judgments from interviews, focus groups, field notes, and emails, with no reported rubric, coder count, or agreement statistic. The page shows the placements as reported (Section 3.3.3) and the evidence the paper cites for each, so a reader can see what each judgment rests on. Coleman's case is the instructive one: the most contact hours, all three participants self-identifying as partners, and the lowest rating, which separates the participants' own use of the word from the framework's.
</div>

## Two cases, nearly a decade apart

<div id="sp-design" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
Click each step to see what happened. The first study followed one urban high school for a school year. The second followed two balloon teams at two rural schools almost a decade later. In both, a researcher sat in on classes, interviewed the partners, and read what they wrote to each other. Use the switch to see who was on each team. The names are pseudonyms.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
A case study looks deeply at a few people instead of measuring many. Here, the cases differ in almost every way: practicing teachers with a graduate engineer in a city school, and college students (engineering, physics, and education majors) in country schools. Notice that each college team had exactly one future teacher, one engineering major, and one physics major. The Cosmic Radiation team taught four lessons in 90-minute classes to students aged 15 to 18; the Speed of Sound team taught five lessons in 50-minute classes to students aged 11 to 14.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
Case 1 data: focus groups with the teachers and the engineer, individual interviews with all three, field notes from 12 full-day classroom observations, and their unmediated emails, coded with grounded theory (open, axial, and selective coding). Case 2 data: individual interviews with each undergraduate and field notes. The school in Case 1 enrolled about 300 students, 90% of them from groups underrepresented in STEM professions and 84% eligible for free or reduced-cost lunch. Case 2 lessons were aligned with the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS); student learning was not assessed.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
The two cases were collected under separate institutional review board protocols (University of Cincinnati, 2009; University of Wyoming, 2019) and differ in participant type, setting, team structure, and funding (the article credits National Science Foundation grant DUE-1821566 in part). The paper reports no counts of interviews, focus groups, or emails, and describes a coding procedure only for Case 1. The researcher's role also differs: an educational liaison inside the Case 1 triad, and an observer who also gave lesson feedback in Case 2. These differences bound what the cross-case comparison can attribute to time.
</div>

## What partners negotiated, and what they never raised

<div id="sp-talk" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
Each row is a moment when partners had to agree on something: a lesson, a schedule, or supplies. Some they settled together, such as dropping an acid demonstration because the room had no fume hood. Others stopped after one message: the engineer suggested using cola instead of acid, and the teacher never answered. The table below the list shows what "partnership" meant to the people involved. Every team talked about making lessons, communicating, and bringing engineering in.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
The researchers define a negotiation as back-and-forth talk between at least two people until they agree. By that definition, few real negotiations happened. Most stopped early: one person made a change, the other did not reply, or an expectation stayed unspoken. The Speed of Sound team even bought parts to test an idea they had already dropped, a compromise nobody named as one. Use the topic switch to compare lessons, time, and supplies.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
The paper finds that "the dialogue was often truncated, and thus the negotiations were at best emerging," centered on lessons, time, and supplies. Coleman's partners used the word "compromise" for their working style, yet the engineer never told the teachers he expected detailed feedback, and one teacher edited his lesson by email without discussion. Of the four themes of partnership meaning, three appear in both cases; the fourth, increasing student learning, appears only in Case 1, because Case 2 did not assess learning.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
The episode list is exhaustive for what the paper reports, and the tags are the page's reading of the paper's wording, flagged for author review. The pattern matters more than the tally: the episodes that reached agreement involved concrete constraints (a missing fume hood, supply lead times, a lesson topic a future teacher could not build), while the ones that stalled involved expectations about roles and feedback. The paper's recommendations target the second kind.
</div>

## The balloon physics one team taught

<div id="sp-balloon" class="w-demo"></div>

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
The Speed of Sound team sent a small 3D-printed box into the sky with a speaker and a tiny computer that timed how long sound took to bounce across it. Drag the altitude slider. The air gets thinner the whole way up, but sound slows down only as far as the air gets colder: about 340 meters per second on the ground and about 295 meters per second where the air bottoms out near −56 °C. Above about 20 kilometers, the air warms again and sound speeds back up, even though the pressure keeps falling.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
After the launch, students graphed the balloon's temperature, pressure, and humidity and connected them to the speed of sound. The chart shows what they could compare against: the standard atmosphere, an average day. Speed of sound follows the temperature curve, not the pressure curve. The box slider shows why timing is hard: across a 20 cm box and back, sound takes about 1,175 microseconds on the ground and about 1,356 at 20 km, a difference of under two ten-thousandths of a second that the payload had to catch.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
In an ideal gas, c = √(γRT/M): the speed of sound depends on temperature and composition, not on pressure, because pressure and density fall together. For dry air (γ = 1.4, R/M = 287.05 J/(kg·K)) that gives 340.3 m/s at 15 °C and 295.1 m/s at −56.5 °C, a 13% drop through the troposphere. Humidity raises the speed slightly (well under 1% near the ground), which is why the team's humidity graph matters less than its temperature graph. The paper reports neither the flight altitude nor the payload's measurements, so the page shows the standard day.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
This demo is the page's own and is not a result of the paper. The engine implements the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere through 47 km (geopotential altitude, five layers) and c = √(γRT) for dry air; it matches the `ambiance` package to within 0.0003% on 189 altitudes and the published layer-boundary pressures (22,632 Pa at 11 km, 5,474.9 Pa at 20 km, and 868.02 Pa at 32 km geopotential) to within 0.0004%. Its place on this page follows the paper's observation that the Speed of Sound team's non-education majors struggled to pitch the physics to students aged 11 to 14; the five reading levels of this demo are one answer to "how far to go down the rabbit hole."
</div>

## What the paper found

<div data-levels="public" markdown="1">
- Only one of the three teams, Cosmic Radiation, reached a real partnership, and none reached the top level.
- The team with the most hours together, Coleman, had the weakest partnership.
- Stronger partnerships made stronger engineering lessons.
- Most disagreements were never fully talked through; people made changes on their own or left expectations unspoken.
- Across nearly a decade, partnerships did not get much better on their own.
</div>

<div data-levels="hs" markdown="1">
- Three teams: one engineer with two teachers (five lessons), and two college teams of three (four and five lessons).
- Cosmic Radiation: weekly meetings, clear roles, trust, and a little-p partnership.
- Speed of Sound: separate work, plans read just before class, and a pre-partnership.
- Coleman: about 20 hours a week together, 12 full days of observed classes, and a pre-partnership.
- What partnership meant: making lessons, communicating, bringing in engineering, and (where measured) helping students learn.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug grad res" markdown="1">
- **Partnership levels.** Cosmic Radiation reached a little-p partnership in most areas; Speed of Sound was pre-partnership with limited little-p aspects; Coleman was the most limited pre-partnership. No team reached big-P Partnership.
- **Time is not the variable.** Coleman's partners spent about 20 hours a week together and reached the lowest level.
- **Partnership meaning was product-centered.** Four themes (lesson creation, communication, engineering content, and student learning), three shared across cases, focused on lessons rather than on the relationship that produced them.
- **Negotiations were truncated.** Few reached back-and-forth agreement; most stalled after one move or were never raised.
- **Partnership strength tracked lesson strength.** Lessons were adequate but did not draw on the partners' full expertise.
- **No growth across a decade.** "Across a decade of time, partnership growth did not significantly improve between these two cases."
</div>

## Why it matters

<div data-levels="public hs" markdown="1">
Bringing an engineer or a college student into a classroom sounds simple, and schools are encouraged to do it. This study shows that putting people in the same room for many hours does not make them partners. The authors recommend four things: agree on roles and expectations at the start, build respect and trust on purpose, make sure the teacher's voice shapes the work, and spend some time on the relationship itself, not only on the lessons.
</div>

<div data-levels="ug" markdown="1">
Engineering in secondary schools often arrives through partnerships, and teachers asked to teach engineering or integrated STEM depend on them. The paper's recommendations are practical and cheap: define expectations and negotiate roles early, cultivate mutual respect with explicit norms, validate and amplify teachers' ideas, and invest in relationship building beyond task completion. The authors connect this to artificial intelligence (AI): as AI tools take on more routine work, the human professional skills of communication and collaboration carry more of the value a partnership adds.
</div>

<div data-levels="grad res" markdown="1">
The study's value is diagnostic. It names the mechanism by which well-resourced partnerships stay shallow (product-centered meaning and truncated negotiation) and points to role agreements and feedback norms as the levers. Its evidence is three teams, analysis by researchers who worked directly with the participants, and data from selected portions of each project year, limitations the authors state. A prospective design that sets role agreements and feedback protocols for some teams and not others, with the same partnership-level judgments made blind to condition, would test the recommendations directly.
</div>

## Notes from building this page

- **Two readings of the levels.** Section 3.3.3 places Cosmic Radiation at a little-p partnership, and the Discussion opens with "Since all the participants functioned mostly in a pre-partnership category." The page reads the Discussion as describing the overall picture and uses the per-team placements from Section 3.3.3.
- **Case 1 dates.** Section 2 describes the first study as "completed in 2011"; Section 3.3 describes its data as "from 2010 to 2011." The page uses 2010–2011, which fits a single school year.
- **Who signed the role agreement.** The Discussion says participants who reached a little-p partnership "signed a written agreement focusing on roles" without naming the team. The page attaches the agreement to Cosmic Radiation, the only team rated little p. This attribution is the page's inference.
- **Coding and counts.** Grounded-theory coding is described for Case 1 only; the abstract's "multi-level coding" is not detailed for Case 2. The paper reports no counts of interviews, focus groups, emails, or coded segments, and no inter-rater or member-checking procedure.
- **"Did not significantly improve."** The decade comparison sets a practicing-teacher triad in an urban school against undergraduate teams in rural schools, under different protocols and funding. "Significantly" here is a qualitative judgment; time, team type, and setting change together, so the comparison cannot isolate change over the decade.
- **Rose's role.** Section 3.2.1 lists Rose as a physics and astrophysics major and later describes her as "the chemistry expert." The page uses the major.
- **Negotiation tags.** The four tags in the negotiation demo (settled together, compromise, truncated, never negotiated) are the page's reading of the paper's descriptions, not codes the paper assigns.
- **The balloon demo.** The standard atmosphere and echo timing are computed by the page as context for the Speed of Sound lesson. The paper reports no flight data.
- **Names.** The article lists its authors as Andrea C. Borowczak, Trina Johnson Kilty, and Mike Borowczak, which the page byline follows. The site's bibliography entry (Andrea C.B. Borowczak, Trina Kilty) is unchanged.

## Try this in class

1. In groups of three, write a one-paragraph role agreement for a short project (for example, building and testing a paper helicopter): who designs, who builds, who explains it to the class, and how the group will give feedback.
2. Do the project. Each person keeps a tally of every disagreement and marks whether the group talked it through, one person decided, or nobody raised it.
3. Compare tallies across groups. Place your group on the three-rung ladder from the first demo, and name one thing in your agreement that helped or one thing you would add.
